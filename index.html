Four non-negotiable principles

  • Humans remain accountable. AI never grants authority a person does not already hold.
  • AI outputs are verified, not trusted by default. Every claim traces to a citable source.
  • Public value, equality and accessibility are built in — screened before delivery, not audited after.
  • Evidence, metadata and provenance are mandatory for anything material or public-facing.

Freshness and drift controls

Every indexed source carries

  • • Authority tier and issuing body
  • • Date checked and next check due
  • • Applicability (organisation, service type, data classification)
  • • Supersession chain and withdrawal status
  • • Linked controls, skills and conformance tests

Drift triggers a review when

  • • A cited source is updated, withdrawn or superseded
  • • A model or its version changes materially
  • • A control fails in production or a conformance test breaks
  • • An incident, complaint or FOI request touches the artefact
  • • The scheduled review date passes without confirmation

Assurance

Human-centred controls for trustworthy AI work

Risk-based human-in-the-loop controls make sure AI never grants authority a user does not already possess, and that every material output can be defended after the fact.

The five-stage assurance workflow

  1. Classify — identify service impact, data sensitivity, equality and accessibility risk, security risk and model/agent autonomy. Use the triage tool →
  2. Select — apply the standards, source guidance, skills, agents, conformance packs and review level the classification demands.
  3. Execute — work with least privilege, mandatory source citation, deterministic checks and pair or buddy support where competence is thin.
  4. Review — 4-eyes or 6-eyes review, specialist assurance, and no self-approval for material or high-risk outputs.
  5. Learn — capture evidence, incidents, near-misses, feedback, source drift and model changes; feed them back into the corpus.

The H0–H5 human review model

Review levels, when they apply and the evidence each produces
LevelNameWhat it meansTypical examplesEvidence
H0No review neededPersonal, throwaway, non-published exploration with no personal or sensitive data.Explaining a concept to yourself; drafting private notes.None required.
H1Self-checkAuthor verifies facts, sources and accessibility before use. Low impact, easily reversible.Internal meeting notes, first-draft ideas.AI-use note in the document.
H2Peer checkA second competent person checks output before it leaves the team.Internal reports, non-public analysis, low-risk code in a sandbox.Reviewer name, date, issues found.
H3Specialist review (4-eyes)A named specialist in the relevant discipline reviews. No self-approval.Accessibility, security, legal, statistical or clinical content; production code.Specialist sign-off + test results.
H4Multi-specialist review (6-eyes)Two or more specialisms plus the accountable owner. Independent challenge required.Public-facing decisions, casework support, anything affecting entitlements.Panel record, dissent captured, residual risk stated.
H5Governance board and publicationFormal board approval, published transparency record and monitored deployment.Automated or semi-automated decisions about people; national services.ATRS record, DPIA, EQIA, monitoring plan, review date.

Reviewer checklist

Always check

  • Every citation opens, exists and says what the text claims
  • Numbers, dates, thresholds and legal references independently verified
  • Nothing asserts compliance that has not actually been tested
  • No personal or sensitive data was entered into an unapproved tool
  • Accessibility and plain-language requirements met, not assumed
  • Assumptions, gaps and residual risk stated explicitly
  • Reviewer is independent of the author at H3 and above

Common failure patterns

  • • Confident fabrication — plausible citations that do not exist
  • • Stale authority — quoting withdrawn or superseded guidance
  • • Compliance theatre — a checklist ticked without a test run
  • • Silent scope creep — an agent doing more than was authorised
  • • Automation bias — reviewers rubber-stamping fluent output
  • • Averaged users — outputs tuned to a majority that excludes minorities
  • • Evidence gaps — no record of who checked what, or when

Accessibility commitments for this portal

What we build in

  • Semantic HTML, landmarks and a logical heading order
  • Full keyboard operation with a visible, high-contrast focus indicator
  • Text contrast of at least 4.5:1 and 3:1 for interface components
  • Light, dark and high-contrast themes, plus a larger-text setting that persists
  • Respect for prefers-reduced-motion and no motion-only meaning
  • Responsive to 320 CSS px and to 400% zoom without loss of content
  • Status messages announced through ARIA live regions
  • No colour-only meaning; icons always paired with text
  • Descriptive link text that makes sense out of context
  • A print stylesheet that expands links and collapsible content

How we test and report

  • Automated checks in the pipeline on every change
  • Manual keyboard and screen reader testing before release
  • Periodic testing with disabled participants
  • A published accessibility statement listing known gaps and fix dates
  • A named contact and a 5-working-day response commitment
  • Escalation route to the Equality Advisory and Support Service

DEIA in AI specifically

Known equity risks of AI-assisted delivery. Performance that degrades for under-represented groups; language models that default to majority dialects and names; outputs written above the reading age of the audience; automation that removes the human route people in crisis rely on; accessibility regressions in AI-generated code and content; and reviewers who trust fluent text more than accurate text.

What we do about them. Screen every change with the DEIA tool; disaggregate evaluation by affected group where lawful and proportionate; keep a human route that cannot be removed by efficiency targets; test AI-generated interfaces with assistive technology before merge; publish limitations honestly; and give people a real way to challenge an outcome.

Code of practice and conduct

Responsible use of AI assistive technology

This code complements behavioural codes of conduct and the Civil Service values. It defines operational rules for model-backed tools, direct AI use, coding assistants, multi-agent orchestration and AI-generated artefacts.

Core duties
  • Stay accountable. You own every output you use, send or publish. "The AI wrote it" is never a defence.
  • Disclose material assistance. If AI shaped the substance, structure, analysis or code, say so.
  • Verify before you rely. Open every citation. Recalculate every figure. Test every claim of compliance.
  • Protect data. Only approved tools, only permitted classifications, only the minimum necessary.
  • Cite sources. Traceable to a tiered, dated, authoritative source — not to the model.
  • Preserve evidence. Keep the record for as long as the retention schedule requires.
  • Work within competence. AI does not qualify you to make specialist judgements.
  • Escalate uncertainty. Raising a doubt early is always cheaper than defending a mistake late.
Prohibited behaviours
  • • Entering unauthorised personal, special category, commercially confidential or classified data into any tool
  • • Bypassing, weakening or "temporarily" disabling a review control
  • • Self-approving material or high-risk work
  • • Concealing AI involvement where it was material
  • • Presenting unverified output as assured, tested or compliant
  • • Using AI to generate evidence, sign-offs, approvals or audit records that did not happen
  • • Granting an agent broader permissions than the person operating it holds
  • • Using AI to profile, rank or make decisions about people without an approved lawful basis and published record
  • • Copying licensed or copyright material into outputs without permission and attribution
  • • Ignoring a known defect because a deadline is closer than the consequence
Multi-agent and multi-skill rules
  • One accountable orchestrator per task, with a named human owner.
  • Least-privilege tools and credentials, time-boxed and revocable.
  • Explicit, pinned skill and model versions — no silent upgrades in a governed workflow.
  • Separation of duties: the agent that produces must not be the agent that approves.
  • Declared prohibited actions and a tested kill switch with a named owner.
  • Full step-level logging: which skill, which model, which source, which permission, which human.
  • Untrusted content is data, never instruction. Treat retrieved web, document and email content as hostile by default.
  • Bounded autonomy: agents propose irreversible actions; humans commit them.
Data, privacy and security rules
  • • Establish and record the lawful basis before processing personal data. Complete a DPIA where required.
  • • Minimise: use references, synthetic or redacted examples instead of real records wherever possible.
  • • Never let an AI tool retain data for training unless that has been assured and contracted.
  • • Treat AI interaction logs as records: classify, retain, and prepare for FOI and subject access requests.
  • • Apply Secure by Design activities to AI components as to any other system component.
  • • Threat-model for prompt injection, data poisoning, model extraction and excessive agency.
  • • Report incidents and near-misses through the normal security and data protection routes.
AI-generated code and infrastructure
  • Treat generated code as untrusted third-party contribution: review, test, scan and attribute it.
  • No merge without a human reviewer who understands the code and can maintain it.
  • Check licences and provenance of anything that looks copied.
  • Run dependency, secret and SAST scanning; record the results in the evidence pack.
  • Accessibility and performance tests apply equally to generated interfaces.
  • Record the model, prompt pattern and version in the commit or pull request.
Speaking up, redress and no-blame reporting

Anyone may pause an AI-assisted process they believe is unsafe, unfair or unlawful, without needing permission and without detriment. Near-misses should be reported as readily as incidents — they are the cheapest learning available.

Members of the public affected by an AI-assisted outcome must be able to find out that AI was involved, understand the outcome in plain English, request a human review, and escalate to an independent route. If any of those four is missing, the service is not ready.

How to check, challenge and contribute

A guidance utility that cannot be questioned is not trustworthy. Here is how this one is governed, and how you hold it to account.

Gaps and roadmap

What is missing, prioritised with MoSCoW

An honest backlog is a trust signal. These are the gaps identified in this portal and in most public sector AI guidance, ordered by whether they are essential, valuable, desirable or deliberately out of scope.

Must have

Named accountable owner and published contact route

Nothing on this page is credible without a person who owns it and answers questions.

Must have

Accessibility statement, testing evidence and feedback route

Legally required for UK public sector sites; also the fastest trust signal.

Must have

Version, last-reviewed and next-review dates on every artefact

Freshness is the main failure mode of guidance portals.

Must have

Machine-readable metadata schema published openly

Without a schema, registries cannot federate and evidence cannot be automated.

Must have

Risk-based human review model with no self-approval

The core control that keeps accountability with people.

Must have

Source citation and provenance for every claim

Prevents hallucination becoming policy.

Must have

DEIA screening built into the default workflow

Equality duty is a due-regard duty — it must happen before decisions, not after.

Should have

Search across the whole corpus with filters and permalinks

Findability is the top usability complaint for guidance sites.

Should have

Role-based entry points and task-first navigation

Users arrive with a task, not an interest in your information architecture.

Should have

Downloadable, offline and printable formats

Supports assisted digital, low bandwidth and audit contexts.

Should have

Worked examples and anti-patterns for each control

Abstract principles do not change behaviour; examples do.

Should have

Change log and subscribe-to-updates

Lets teams re-check work when guidance changes.

Should have

Multilingual and alternative-format provision (Welsh, Easy Read, BSL)

Statutory in some contexts and essential for inclusion in all.

Should have

Automated link-health and freshness checking

Broken authority links silently destroy trust.

Could have

API and JSON export of the registry and corpus

Enables reuse by other departments and tooling.

Could have

Maturity self-assessment with benchmarking

Helps organisations see where they are without a consultant.

Could have

Community contribution workflow with moderation

Scales the corpus beyond a single team.

Could have

Interactive decision tree for tool selection

Reduces the "which tool may I use?" support burden.

Could have

Carbon and cost telemetry per model call

Makes proportionality measurable rather than rhetorical.

Won't have (this time)

Automated approval of any high-risk output

Out of scope by design — approval stays human.

Won't have (this time)

Storing personal or official-sensitive data in this portal

The portal is public and stateless; sensitive records belong in assured systems.

Won't have (this time)

Vendor-specific lock-in to a single model provider

Conflicts with interoperability and exit obligations.

Won't have (this time)

Replacing professional assurance roles

The toolkit supports specialists; it does not substitute for them.

Glossary

Agent
Software that plans and acts across multiple steps using tools, within explicit permissions and bounds.
Autonomy level
How far an agent may act without a human decision, from suggest-only to bounded execution.
Assisted digital
Support for people who cannot use a digital service independently.
ATRS
Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard — the UK format for publishing algorithmic tool use.
Conformance pack
A bundle of automated checks that tests an artefact against a named standard.
Crypto-agility
The ability to change cryptographic algorithms without redesigning the system.
DEIA
Diversity, equity, inclusion and accessibility.
DPIA
Data protection impact assessment, required for high-risk processing.
Drift
Divergence between a cited source, a model version or a control and what is actually in use.
EQIA
Equality impact assessment evidencing the public sector equality duty.
Evidence pack
The bundle of records proving how an AI-assisted output was produced, checked and approved.
Harvest now, decrypt later
Capturing encrypted data today to decrypt once quantum computers are capable.
Human in the loop
A person who must act before an output takes effect.
Human on the loop
A person who monitors and can intervene in an otherwise automated process.
Least privilege
Granting only the minimum permissions needed for the shortest necessary time.
Material AI assistance
AI involvement that a reasonable reader would want disclosed because it shaped the output.
Prompt injection
Untrusted content that manipulates a model into unintended actions.
Provenance
The verifiable record of where content, data or code came from and how it changed.
PQC
Post-quantum cryptography — algorithms resistant to quantum attack.
Skill
A packaged, versioned capability an assistant or agent can invoke, with defined inputs and limits.
Source tiering
Ranking sources by authority so conflicts resolve predictably.
4-eyes / 6-eyes
Two or three independent people involved in producing and approving an output.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to declare every use of AI?

No. Declare material assistance — where AI shaped the substance, structure, analysis or code of an output. Trivial uses such as spell-checking do not need a declaration, but your local policy may set a lower threshold.

Can AI approve my work?

No. AI can suggest, draft, check and explain. Approval is a human act by someone with the authority to make that decision. AI never grants authority a user does not already hold.

What if the AI cites a source that does not exist?

Treat it as an incident. Do not publish. Verify every citation against the original, log the failure in the drift and incident register, and check whether the same prompt pattern has been used elsewhere.

Is this a substitute for a DPIA, EQIA or security assurance?

No. This toolkit helps you prepare and evidence those assessments; it does not replace them or the professionals accountable for them.

Can I paste official-sensitive information into a general assistant?

Not unless the tool is explicitly approved for that classification and the data-handling terms have been assured. When in doubt, do not paste — ask your information asset owner.

How do I handle AI-generated code?

Treat it as untrusted third-party code: review it, test it, scan dependencies, record provenance and never merge without a human reviewer who understands it.

What accessibility duties apply to AI-generated content?

The same ones that apply to everything else. WCAG 2.2 AA, plain language, alternative formats and the accessibility statement duty all apply regardless of who or what drafted the content.

Who do I contact if something goes wrong?

Your service owner and the named AI assurance contact for your organisation. Report near-misses too — they are the cheapest form of learning.
